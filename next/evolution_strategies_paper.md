# arXiv 2511.16652 ("Evolution Strategies at the Hyperscale" / EGGROLL) and its fit for browser-swarm training

Research date: 18 November 2026. Anything dated earlier is marked with its date.

Plain statement of what the paper is: the suspicion was correct. arXiv 2511.16652 is an evolution strategies (ES) paper - backprop-free, forward-pass-only training. It introduces EGGROLL, a low-rank-perturbation variant of OpenAI-style ES, aimed at making ES fast on GPUs for very large models. It is NOT a paper about peer-to-peer, federated, browser or volunteer training. Its distributed framework is a trusted coordinator-worker cluster. The parts that matter for a browser swarm are (a) the seeds-plus-scalar-fitness communication pattern it inherits from Salimans et al. 2017, (b) its pure-integer (int8) model and integer update rule, and (c) its ternary fitness packing at 1.6 bits per value.

How I read the sources: the main text was read through the arXiv HTML page; the appendices (E to N) were read directly from the v2 PDF text because the HTML fetch truncated before them. Salimans et al. 2017 was read directly from its PDF. Most follow-up papers were read at abstract level only - this is flagged per item.

## 1. Title, authors, venue, and the core method at pseudo-code level. What is computed per worker, what is communicated, bytes per update?

### Takeaway
"Evolution Strategies at the Hyperscale" (Sarkar, Fellows, Duque et al., Oxford FLAIR/WhiRL, Mila, NVIDIA; ICML 2026) perturbs each weight matrix with a rank-r product (1/sqrt(r)) A B^T instead of a dense Gaussian matrix, evaluates fitness with forward passes only, and updates with the fitness-weighted sum of perturbations. Workers exchange only scalar fitness values (noise is regenerated from shared seeds with a counter-based RNG); in the paper's own distributed framework each fitness is a ternary value packed at 1.6 bits, giving a payload of about 52 + chunk_size/10 bytes per chunk, independent of model size.

### Cited Findings
- Title: "Evolution Strategies at the Hyperscale". Method name: EGGROLL = "Evolution Guided GeneRal Optimisation via Low-rank Learning". arXiv:2511.16652 [cs.LG]; v2 is dated 16 Feb 2026 (v1 was November 2025). - [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16652v2)
- Authors (20): Bidipta Sarkar*, Mattie Fellows*, Juan Agustin Duque* (equal contribution); Alistair Letcher, Antonio Leon Villares, Anya Sims, Clarisse Wibault, Dmitry Samsonov, Dylan Cope, Jarek Liesen, Kang Li, Lukas Seier, Theo Wolf, Uljad Berdica, Valentin Mohl (core contributors); Alexander David Goldie, Aaron Courville, Karin Sevegnani; Shimon Whiteson and Jakob Nicolaus Foerster (equal senior). Affiliations: FLAIR and WhiRL at University of Oxford, Mila - Quebec AI Institute, NVIDIA AI Technology Center, CIFAR AI Chair, NormaCore.dev. - [arXiv PDF v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Venue: Proceedings of the 43rd International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML 2026), PMLR volume 306, pages 107670 to 107741. OpenReview forum id bfVJ4GsHrO. Software link given by PMLR: github.com/ESHyperscale/HyperscaleES. - [PMLR v306](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/sarkar26a.html)
- Core idea: instead of sampling a full-rank perturbation E in R^(m x n), sample A in R^(m x r) and B in R^(n x r) with r much smaller than min(m, n) and form E = (1/sqrt(r)) A B^T. Auxiliary perturbation storage drops from mn to (m+n)r per layer. - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Algorithm 1 as printed in the paper (read directly from the PDF): "initialise M and workers with known random seeds; for Tmax timesteps: for each worker i in parallel: A_i ~ p(A_i), B_i ~ p(B_i); E_i <- (1/sqrt(r)) A_i B_i^T; f_i <- f(W = M + sigma E_i); workers share scalar fitness f_i with other workers; for each worker i in parallel: reconstruct E_j for all j from the seeds; M <- M + alpha (1/N_workers) sum_j E_j f_j". - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 4.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- The paper states it "adapt[s] the parallelised evolutionary strategies algorithm from Salimans et al. (2017)" - the seed-sharing scheme is inherited, not new. The 1/sigma factor is absorbed into the learning rate. - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 4.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- The update is not low-rank: summing N rank-r perturbations gives an update of rank min(Nr, m, n) almost surely, and "for all experiments in Section 6, N r > min(m, n)", i.e. updates are full-rank. - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 4.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Per-worker forward pass for a linear layer: u(M + sigma E)^T = u M^T + (sigma/sqrt(r)) (u B) A^T. With r = 1 the extra work is one dot product of length d_in and one scalar-times-vector per layer. This is the same decomposition as batched LoRA inference. - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 4.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Update computation for r = 1: stack A in R^(N x d_out), B in R^(N x d_in) and compute (diag(f) A)^T B, one matrix multiply, without materialising individual E_i. - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 4.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Noise is never stored or sent: "we use a counter-based deterministic random number generator (RNG) (Salmon et al., 2011; Bradbury et al., 2018) to reconstruct noise on demand, so matrix perturbations need not persist in memory". - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- The official JAX repo seeds noise from an (epoch, thread_id) tuple (as summarised from its README). - [HyperscaleES README](https://github.com/ESHyperscale/HyperscaleES)
- Score function: the paper uses a Gaussian approximate score (S(E) = -E), justified because the distribution of E approaches Gaussian as r grows; alternative mean-field approximators (Appendix D) performed worse overall. - [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16652v2)
- Communication in the paper's own distributed framework (Appendix J, read directly): "Since workers evaluate perturbations in antithetic pairs, the raw signal is discretised into ternary values {+1, 0, -1}. These are mapped to {0, 1, 2} and packed five at a time into a single byte" - "an effective bitrate of 1.6 bits per value (near the log2 3 = 1.585 theoretical limit). Consequently, the network payload per chunk is approximately 52 + chunk_size/10 bytes, rendering bandwidth usage independent of model size." - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix J.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Topology of that framework: "The system employs a Coordinator-Worker topology. The Coordinator maintains the global state and assigns population chunks to Workers. Workers calculate fitness on GPU, apply signal shaping (chunk mean filtering, adaptive thresholding), and return only the packed ternary fitness". - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix J.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Integer variant, per worker (Appendix H, read directly): rank-1 perturbation vectors A, B are int8, sampled as round(16 x standard normal), taken by random indexing into a pre-generated table; the perturbed layer computes I8((u theta^T + ((u . B) A >> (4 + sigma_hat))) / (16 sqrt(n))), with sigma = 2^(-sigma_hat). Fitness shaping is sign(s_plus - s_minus) per antithetic pair, so shaped fitness is in {-1, 0, 1}. - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix H.1 and H.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Integer variant, update (Appendix H.3, read directly): compute E = (diag(F) A)^T B in int32; where |E| exceeds a threshold, move that int8 weight by exactly one bin in the direction sign(E). The threshold is a z-test style value scaled by 16 x 16 x sqrt(N) and a normal quantile set by a hyperparameter alpha in (0, 1); "alpha approximately equals the fraction of parameters that are updated at each step". No momentum or optimiser state. - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix H.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)

### Inferences
- Bytes per step, derived from Appendix J: one ternary value per antithetic pair at 1.6 bits is about 0.8 bits per population member. A peer that evaluates one antithetic pair per step needs under 2 bits of payload; a chunk of 1,000 members is about 152 bytes including the roughly 52-byte header; the whole 2^20 population is about 105 KB per step. The paper gives the formula, not these worked numbers.
- No seed has to travel at all if the swarm agrees on (base key, step number, member index) -> noise. Only the member index assignment and the fitness need to be known. This matches both Algorithm 1 ("known random seeds") and the repo's (epoch, thread_id) keying.
- Hidden per-peer cost that the "tiny message" framing obscures: every worker that holds the model must reconstruct ALL N perturbations each step to apply the update. For r = 1 that is N x (m + n) random draws plus an (m x N) by (N x n) matrix product per layer, i.e. O(N x m x n) multiply-adds per layer per step, on every peer. Communication is O(N) bits; compute is O(N x d).
- The paper's headline contribution (arithmetic intensity on GPUs when batching thousands of perturbations on one device) is largely irrelevant to a browser peer evaluating one or two perturbations of a small model. What transfers is: cheaper noise generation ((m+n)r draws instead of mn per matrix), the integer model plus integer update rule, and the ternary fitness encoding.
- The coordinator in Appendix J "maintains the global state". A serverless swarm would have to replace it with a deterministic rule that every peer can evaluate (who evaluates which member index at which step, and which fitness set closes a step). The paper offers nothing for this.
- The formula for the integer threshold is hard to read from PDF text extraction; my reading is threshold = 16 x 16 x sqrt(N) x Phi^-1(1 - alpha/2), a two-sided test. Check against the nano-egg source before relying on it.

### Gaps
- The exact contents of the roughly 52-byte per-chunk header are not specified in the paper.
- The "signal shaping (chunk mean filtering, adaptive thresholding)" applied by workers is named but not specified in the paper; it would have to be read from code.
- I could not access the OpenReview forum (bot check), so reviewer discussion of the method is not covered.

## 2. Headline results with numbers: model sizes, tasks, population sizes, throughput, comparison with backprop. Integer-only, quantized, recurrent, non-differentiable results?

### Takeaway
The paper's strongest results are for fine-tuning billion-parameter recurrent LLMs with outcome-only rewards (competitive with or better than GRPO at equal wall-clock) and for throughput (91 percent of pure batch inference). The result most relevant here - from-scratch training of a pure-int8 recurrent language model with roughly 4.9M parameters - works and slightly beats a backprop baseline, but only with a population of 2^20 and about 180 times the GPU-hours.

### Cited Findings
- Throughput: EGGROLL reaches 91 on a scale where 100 is maximum batch-inference throughput (69 when noise is regenerated on the fly with the JAX PRNG); PPO is 34; OpenES is 0.41 (0.054 with on-the-fly noise). Measured on one GH200 (equivalent to one H100) "for a linear model with dimension 8192 in bfloat16", maximum batch size 1024. - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix E, Figure 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- The abstract's claim is "a hundredfold increase in training speed for billion-parameter models at large population sizes". - [arXiv PDF v2, abstract](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Arithmetic intensity argument: dense Gaussian ES has intensity strictly below 1 op/byte regardless of batch size; EGGROLL with m = 8192, r = 1 reaches the H100 roofline of about 300 ops/byte at batch 352, versus 324 for plain batched inference. Overhead relative to inference is (4r+1)/(2m) of compute. - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix F](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Integer-only pretraining: the EGG ("Evolved Generative GRU") model, 6 layers, hidden dimension 256, all weights int8, all activations integer, no activation functions (nonlinearity comes from int8 saturation), character-level on MiniPile. Parameters updated after 100 tokens per population member, "truncated ES" that keeps the hidden state. Best test loss 3.40 bits/byte versus 3.58 for a dense 6L-256D fp32 Transformer trained with backprop SGD on the same data batch size (16 sequences). - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 6.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Cost of that result: "our largest population size of 2^20 = 1048576 requires around 180 times more GPU-hours than the backprop baseline". The authors frame this as "compute-only scaling in limited data regimes". - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 6.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- EGG speed: about 10M tokens/s on one H100 at population 2^20. - [Project page](https://eshyperscale.github.io/)
- EGG parameter count formula: 513D + l(4D + 12D^2) for hidden size D and l layers; the embedding and head are 256 x D (byte vocabulary). - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix G.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Reference points for the 3.40 figure: the nano-egg README lists unigram at 5.00/4.91 and bigram at 3.97/3.90 bits per byte (train/validation). - [nano-egg README](https://github.com/ESHyperscale/nano-egg)
- Tabula rasa RL: 16 environments (Navix, Craftax, Brax, Kinetix, Jumanji), 3-layer MLP policies with 256 hidden units, 10 seeds, per-environment hyperparameter search. EGGROLL is competitive with OpenES on 7/16, underperforms on 2/16, outperforms on 7/16. - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 6.2 and Appendix N.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Multi-agent RL: three cooperative MPE tasks with feed-forward networks of width 64 and depth 3; chosen population sizes were 128, 512 and 4096 depending on task, rank 1. - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix N.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- LLM fine-tuning, Countdown, RWKV-7 1.5B, one GPU, equal wall-clock: 35 percent validation accuracy versus 23 percent for GRPO. Figure caption: 1024 parallel generations per GPU (618 updates) for EGGROLL versus 64 (915 updates) for GRPO. - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 6.3, Figure 4b](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Countdown, RWKV-7 7B on 8 GPUs: 72.9 percent validation accuracy, versus 52.8 percent (GRPO) and 66.8 percent (OpenES) as reported by Qiu et al. 2025 on Qwen2.5-7B. The authors caution that Qiu et al. do not report hardware or wall-clock, so the comparison is not controlled. - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix N.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- GSM8K, RWKV-7 7B, 8 GPUs: EGGROLL with 8192 parallel generations (260 updates) outperforms GRPO with 256 (340 updates). - [arXiv PDF v2, Figure 5a](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- RWKV-7 14B on DeepScaleR, 5000-token thinking budget, 32 GPUs for 12 hours: AIME24 13 to 30 percent, AIME25 7 to 33 percent, HMMT25 11 to 13 percent. GRPO is described as infeasible at this size because of Adam's memory. - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 6.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Transformer check (Qwen3-4B-Base on DeepScaleR, via vLLM, population 2048, LoRA rank 1): average over five maths benchmarks 28.0 base, 41.4 with EGGROLL, 41.4 with RL (RL numbers taken from Liu et al. 2025). Per benchmark it is mixed: MATH500 75.8 vs 67.4 in EGGROLL's favour, MinervaMath 31.3 vs 40.1 against. - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix L.1, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Non-differentiable objective: pass@k (K = 4) optimised directly on Qwen3-1.7B with population 256, LoRA rank 1; answer diversity rises under pass@k and collapses under pass@1. - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix L.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Quantised fine-tuning: RWKV-7 7B quantised to int8 (symmetric per-channel absmax scaling to [-127, 127]), then distilled from the unquantised model on GSM8K with a KL fitness, using EGGROLL plus Adam; Adam's real-valued proposal is z-scored per tensor and converted into a sparse unit step in {-1, 0, +1}. The quantised model starts unable to solve any validation problem and progressively recovers some ability. - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 6.4 and Appendix K](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Recurrent models: RWKV-7 is chosen because its constant state size frees memory for population members; EGG is a nonlinear RNN trained without backprop through time "on unbounded sequence lengths". - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 6.3 and Appendix G.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Other domain: a finance time-series world model fine-tuned into a high-frequency trading agent optimising PnL directly. - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix M](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)

### Inferences
- EGG 6L-256D parameter count, computed from the paper's formula: 513 x 256 + 6 x (4 x 256 + 12 x 256^2) = 4,856,064 int8 parameters, about 4.9 MB. The paper does not print this number.
- The only from-scratch supervised result in the paper is the EGG experiment, and its sample efficiency is poor: equal data, about 180 times the compute, for a gain of 0.18 bits/byte over a small SGD baseline. The baseline is "backprop SGD" at batch 16, not a tuned Adam run, so "beats backprop" should be read narrowly. 3.40 bits/byte is 0.5 below the bigram baseline - a real but modest language model.
- At roughly 10M tokens/s and 100 tokens per member per step, one step at population 2^20 is on the order of 10 seconds on an H100. A swarm of browsers would need a very large aggregate forward-pass throughput to reproduce that regime; smaller populations on a smaller model are the realistic target.
- The LLM results are fine-tuning with outcome rewards, where the comparison is against RL (GRPO), not against supervised backprop. They say little about from-scratch training of a small model.

### Gaps
- Test loss per population size (Figure 2b) is only available as a plot; I could not extract the population size at which EGG crosses the backprop baseline.
- Internal inconsistencies in the paper: the Countdown figure caption says 1024 parallel generations per GPU while Table 10 says 1536; Appendix N.3 says "8096 v.s. 512" where the tables say 8192 and 256; the 7B hard-maths run is "128 GPUs for 24 hours" in the text and "128 GPUs for 12 hours" in the Figure 5b caption.
- No result in the paper for contrastive objectives, embedding models, or vision models.

## 3. Stated limitations and failure modes. Which hyperparameters matter? What population size is needed before it works at all?

### Takeaway
The paper has no limitations section; the practical limits are implied: from-scratch training needs very large populations (population 2 "significantly underperforms"), sigma must shrink with dimension or the update diverges, and the integer trainer has no momentum. Independent follow-ups in 2026 add catastrophic forgetting, a possible stability reversal from rank-one perturbations, and a rise-then-decay reward pattern under fixed hyperparameters.

### Cited Findings
- "We note that large population sizes are critical for pretraining; a population size of 2, analogous to MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023), significantly underperforms larger population sizes despite having access to the same data batch." - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 6.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Related-work statement: "we find that large population sizes are necessary for pretraining, indicating such [two-point zeroth-order] methods are unsuitable here." - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 3.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Full-rank updates require the population to be at least the hidden dimension (N r at least min(m, n)). - [Project page, footnote 1](https://eshyperscale.github.io/)
- Sigma regimes (theory): with sigma_d = o(d^(-1/2)) Gaussian ES linearises and recovers the local gradient at rate Theta((sigma_d sqrt(d))^alpha); at sigma_d of order d^(-1/2) higher-order terms remain; above that, there exist smooth cubic objectives for which the update norm grows as Theta(sigma_d^2 d) and diverges. "In practice, sigma_d is often absorbed into the ES update stepsize, and its scale is adjusted automatically as part of the hyperparameter regime to ensure stability." - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 5.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Rank: low-rank updates converge to full-rank Gaussian ES at O(1/r), and to the true ES gradient as d grows for any fixed r including 1. "Even at r = 1, the function is not a poor approximation. After r = 10, the function has nearly converged". The conclusion claims rank 1 gives "negligible decrease in performance". - [arXiv PDF v2, Sections 5.2, 5.3 and 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Sigma values actually used span three orders of magnitude: 7e-4 (Countdown, 1.5B, population 256, learning-rate scale 0.125, plain gradient descent), 2e-3 (GSM8K, 7B, 8192 parallel generations, learning-rate scale 0.06), 1e-3 (Qwen via vLLM, learning rate 1e-3), 0.4 (int8-quantised 7B, population 8192, learning-rate scale 3e-7), and 0.05 to 0.5 for small RL MLPs. - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix N, Tables 10, 12, 14, 15 and Appendix L Table 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Larger populations allow a larger sigma: sigma was raised from 7e-4 to 2e-3 "as the significantly bigger population sizes ... allow for much more stable training and aggressive exploration". - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix N.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- RL search space (small MLPs): pop_size in {512, 1024, 2048, 4096}; rank in {1, 2, 4}; optimizer in {adamw, sgd, adam}; learning_rate in {1e-3, 1e-2, 1e-1}; sigma in {0.05, 0.2, 0.5}; sigma_decay and lr_decay in {0.995, 0.999, 0.9995, 1.0}; rank_transform on or off; 20 random-search trials per environment. Selected ranks varied by environment (1, 2 and 4 all appear). - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix N.4, Table 15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Integer EGG settings: "Across model sizes and population size, we find that setting sigma_hat to 4 and letting alpha decay over training steps as 1/(0.015t + 1) gives consistently strong results." - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix H.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Missing optimiser state: "We currently do not incorporate any momentum or other optimiser states, but this remains critical future work to improve the speed of convergence for pure integer training." - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix H.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Data batch versus population (EGG ablation): "the minimum batch size for near-optimal performance at a given population size N appears to be N/4^6"; "large population sizes need larger data batches". When the batch b exceeds N/2, each antithetic pair gets 2b/N sequences. - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix I](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Authors' own framing: the project post calls itself an early research checkpoint; the official repo README calls the code "a research preview". - [Project page](https://eshyperscale.github.io/); [HyperscaleES README](https://github.com/ESHyperscale/HyperscaleES)
- Independent analysis (Kaya and Hashemi, Sept 2026, abstract only): rank-one perturbations "occupy a zero-volume subset of the matrix space"; at finite rank and nonzero radius the EGGROLL mean-field update acts as a resolvent on the gradient of a smoothed objective, which "can make the mean-field update nonconservative and can reverse the local stability of an optimum". On quadratics the gradient is still recovered exactly. Excess sampling variance of rank-one relative to dense Gaussian ES decays inversely with matrix width and is 0.098 percent at width 4096. - [arXiv 2609.10980](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10980)
- Same paper proposes LOO-ROLL, a leave-one-out estimator using one evaluation per direction instead of an antithetic pair; reported matched-time gains over EGGROLL of +2.9, +14.1, +7.9 points on GSM8K at 1.7B, 8B, 14B, with 11 significant improvements and no significant losses over 14 settings. - [arXiv 2609.10980](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10980)
- Catastrophic forgetting (Abdi et al., Jan 2026, abstract only): ES approaches GRPO on maths and reasoning at comparable compute but with significant forgetting of prior abilities; ES updates are much less sparse and have L2 norms "orders of magnitude larger" than GRPO updates. - [arXiv 2601.20861](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20861)
- Rise-then-decay (Liang et al., Jan 2026, abstract only): under fixed hyperparameters, fine-tuning reward "often rises, peaks, and then degrades in both ES and GRPO"; small populations (N about 30) suffice for billion-parameter fine-tuning because improvement concentrates in a few high-curvature directions. Tested on Qwen2.5 0.5B to 7B only. - [arXiv 2602.00170](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00170)
- "ES needs a smaller population size for a larger LLM" (Ba et al., Aug 2026, abstract only). - [arXiv 2608.27351](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27351)
- Classical caveat from the prior art (2017): for general non-smooth problems "the required number of optimization steps scales linearly with the dimension", though what matters is the intrinsic dimension of the problem, not the raw parameter count. - [Salimans et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.03864)

### Inferences
- Minimum workable population, pieced together: (a) 2 is not enough for from-scratch training; (b) N r must reach the layer width for full-rank updates, so a 256-wide model at rank 1 wants at least 256 members per step; (c) the smallest populations the paper actually selected were 128 (width-64 MARL nets), 256 (LLM fine-tuning) and 512 (RL search floor). A plausible floor for a small from-scratch model is therefore a few hundred members per step, with quality improving up to very large values. This is my synthesis, not a stated threshold.
- Small models need a comparatively LARGER sigma and LARGER population than large ones: the theory scales sigma with d^(-1/2), the tables show sigma around 0.05 to 0.5 for 10^4 to 10^5-parameter MLPs versus 1e-3 for LLMs, and Ba et al. report population need falling with model size. The "small populations suffice" results come from fine-tuning pretrained billion-parameter models and should not be assumed for training a sub-1M model from scratch.
- The rank-one variance penalty is small even for narrow layers if the 1/width law from Kaya and Hashemi extrapolates: 0.098 percent at 4096 implies roughly 1.6 percent at width 256 and 6 percent at width 64. That is an extrapolation of an abstract-level claim under their local affine model.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity is real: the RL results rely on 20-trial per-environment search over nine hyperparameters, and chosen values differ widely between environments. A swarm cannot easily run such sweeps, so sigma decay and learning-rate decay schedules would need to be fixed in advance (the EGG rule sigma_hat = 4, alpha = 1/(0.015t+1) is the only "works across sizes" recipe the paper offers).
- The data-batch finding (minimum batch about N/4096) suggests that for populations up to a few thousand, a single shared sequence per step was near-optimal in their setup. For a swarm this means the shared-data requirement per step can be tiny.

### Gaps
- No ablation in the paper isolates population size for the fine-tuning tasks, or rank for the EGG model.
- The follow-up claims above (Kaya and Hashemi, Abdi et al., Liang et al., Ba et al.) are from abstracts; I did not read their full experiments.
- OpenReview reviews for the ICML 2026 paper could not be fetched.

## 4. Is there official code? Any JavaScript, WASM, or WebGPU ports? Replications, critiques, follow-ups through November 2026?

### Takeaway
Official code is JAX-only (HyperscaleES, plus the single-file nano-egg int8 trainer), both GPL-3.0 and both labelled research previews. There is one browser implementation I could find - Tapasya, plain JavaScript plus WGSL/WebGPU with a CPU fallback and no ML framework - which trains a roughly 130K-parameter transformer with EGGROLL in a tab, single-user, with no published benchmarks. A sizeable 2026 follow-up literature exists, mostly on LLM post-training.

### Cited Findings
- Official library: github.com/ESHyperscale/HyperscaleES - JAX, GPL-3.0, described as "a research preview"; 386 stars, 43 forks, 7 commits, 2 open issues as displayed on 18 Nov 2026. Contains a Noiser abstraction, a Model class, an end-to-end test that trains an MLP, and LLM evolution scripts. No hyperparameter guidance and no ports listed. - [HyperscaleES](https://github.com/ESHyperscale/HyperscaleES)

## 5. Prior art for the same communication trick: shared-seed distributed ES, zeroth-order federated learning with seeds and scalars, forward-gradient methods. Concrete communication costs and accuracy figures.

### Takeaway
The seeds-plus-scalars trick dates to Salimans et al. 2017 and has been reused heavily in federated zeroth-order work since 2023: FedKSeed (under 18 KB per round for billion-parameter models), DeComFL (about 1 MB total), SeedFlood (400 KB total per edge, fully decentralised, 4 to 6 percent below gradient-based DSGD). All of the large-model results are fine-tuning; from-scratch zeroth-order results exist for small CNNs (DeepZero: ResNet-20 at 86.94 percent on CIFAR-10).

### Cited Findings
- Salimans et al. 2017 (OpenAI ES), Algorithm 2: "Initialize: n workers with known random seeds"; each worker samples its perturbation, computes its return, "Send all scalar returns F_i from each worker to every other worker", then every worker "Reconstruct[s] all perturbations ... using known random seeds" and applies theta <- theta + alpha (1/(n sigma)) sum_j F_j eps_j. - [Salimans et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.03864)
- Same paper: "each worker only needs to communicate a single scalar to and from each other worker to agree on a parameter update"; "the only information that needs to be communicated across processes are the scalar return and the random seed". - [Salimans et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.03864)
- Implementation detail: each worker instantiates "a large block of Gaussian noise at the start of training" and perturbs by "a randomly indexed subset" of it; update reconstruction "only takes up a small fraction of total time ... even when using up to 1,440 parallel workers"; with more workers or larger networks, workers can perturb only a subset of parameters. - [Salimans et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.03864)
- Results (2017): 3D Humanoid solved in 10 minutes on 1,440 CPU cores versus 657 minutes on 18 cores; Atari competitive after one hour on 720 CPUs; ES needed "between 3x and 10x as much data" as A3C on Atari, under 10x sample penalty versus TRPO on hard MuJoCo tasks and up to 3x better on simple ones. MuJoCo policies were MLPs with two 64-unit hidden layers. - [Salimans et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.03864)
- Techniques used: antithetic (mirrored) sampling; rank-transformation fitness shaping, which "removes the influence of outlier individuals in each population"; weight decay; virtual batch normalisation; fixed sigma. Skipping backprop reduces "computation per episode by about two thirds". - [Salimans et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.03864)
- EGGROLL's own comparison: Salimans et al. "use a maximum population size of 1440, whereas we use over a million". - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 3.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Deep Neuroevolution (Such et al., Dec 2017): a genetic algorithm evolving networks with over four million parameters, with an encoding technique giving up to 10,000-fold compression; Atari in about 1 hour on 720 cores. - [arXiv 1712.06567](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.06567)
- MeZO (Malladi et al., NeurIPS 2023 oral): in-place zeroth-order SGD with inference-level memory; trains a 30B model on one A100 80GB where backprop fits only 2.7B; up to 12x memory reduction; "comparable performance to fine-tuning with backpropagation"; can optimise non-differentiable objectives such as accuracy or F1; theory says adequate pre-training and task prompts are what make it work. - [arXiv 2305.17333](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17333)
- FedKSeed (Qin et al., ICML 2024; arXiv Dec 2023): each local step samples one of K candidate seeds and one data instance, computes the scalar [L(w + eps z) - L(w - eps z)] / (2 eps) with two forward passes, updates, and logs (seed, scalar). After 200 local steps the client uploads its seed-scalar history; the server keeps K scalar accumulators and sends the K seeds plus K accumulators, never model parameters. - [FedKSeed v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.06353v5)
- FedKSeed cost: 17,988 bytes per round with K = 4096 (9,796 bytes for the Pro variant with K = 1024), versus 5.01 GB (DataJuicer-1.3B) and 12.76 GB (LLaMA-3B) for FedAvg and 12.00 MB / 20.31 MB for LoRA-based FedIT. Each seed is encoded in 4 bytes. Peak GPU memory 3.5 GB / 7.8 GB versus 17.8 GB / 39.1 GB for FedAvg. - [FedKSeed v5, Table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.06353v5)
- FedKSeed accuracy (Rouge-L): Natural Instructions, LLaMA-3B: 29.77 (FedKSeed) and 30.19 (Pro) versus 27.88 (FedAvg) and 28.13 (FedIT); Dolly-15K alpha = 0.5, LLaMA-3B: 35.64 and 36.29 versus 34.27 and 33.23. Average relative improvement 7.26 percent over the best practical baseline. - [FedKSeed v5, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.06353v5)
- DeComFL (Li et al., ICLR 2025; first version May 2024 under the name FedDisco): per-round communication reduced from O(d) to O(1), "transmitting only a constant number of scalar values between clients and the server in each round"; "around 1MB of data in total between the server and a client" to fine-tune a model with billions of parameters. - [arXiv 2405.15861](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15861)
- DeComFL/FedDisco accuracy (v1): OPT-125M on SST-2: SGD 86.40, MeZO 84.90, P = 1 84.69, P = 5 85.16, P = 10 86.73 percent. Small from-scratch models were also used: an MNIST CNN with 28,938 parameters and a Fashion-MNIST CNN with 1,206,590 parameters; the method converged more slowly than first-order methods there. - [FedDisco v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15861v1)
- SeedFlood (Kim and Lee, POSTECH, Feb 2026): decentralised (no server) zeroth-order training where each update is a (seed, scalar) pair flooded to all clients; clients filter seen messages, log new ones, and apply them. Total communication per edge: SeedFlood 400 KB versus DSGD 526.3 GB, ChocoSGD 15.79 GB, DSGD-LoRA 629.1 MB, Choco-LoRA 18.8 MB. - [SeedFlood](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.18181v1)
- SeedFlood accuracy: with 16 clients fine-tuning OPT-1.3B, average relative performance versus DSGD is -4.13 percent (ring) and -5.98 percent (meshgrid). With OPT-125M at 128 clients on a ring, SeedFlood scores 100.24 (normalised to 16-client DSGD) versus 94.50 for DSGD. 5,000 zeroth-order iterations; perturbations restricted to a shared low-rank subspace (rank 32 or 64) refreshed every 1,000 or 5,000 iterations. - [SeedFlood](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.18181v1)
- BAFFLE (Feng et al., ECCV 2024; arXiv Jan 2023): backprop-free federated learning where clients run only forward passes and "return a set of scalars to the server"; claims compatibility with inference-only hardware, quantisation and trusted execution environments; "acceptable results" from scratch and when fine-tuning. - [arXiv 2301.12195](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12195)
- DeepZero (Chen et al., ICLR 2024): zeroth-order training from scratch; ResNet-20 on CIFAR-10 reaches 86.94 percent test accuracy, using coordinate-wise gradient estimation, sparsity, feature reuse and forward parallelisation. - [arXiv 2310.02025](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.02025v4)
- HiSo ("Converge Faster, Talk Less", ICLR 2026) and DistZO2 (ACL Findings 2026) are further federated/distributed zeroth-order methods sharing only scalars and seeds, per a curated list; not opened. - [Awesome-Gradient-Free-LLMOpt](https://github.com/zz1358m/Awesome-Gradient-Free-LLMOpt)

### Inferences
- A raw float32 fitness is 4 bytes per member per step in the Salimans scheme (the paper gives no byte count). EGGROLL's ternary antithetic encoding cuts that to 0.2 bytes per pair. FedKSeed's 18 KB is dominated by resending the seed list (4096 x 4 bytes = 16,384 bytes); a swarm that derives seeds from a counter needs none of that.
- Two families share the wire format but differ in substance: (a) ES proper (Salimans, EGGROLL) - one synchronous population per step, fitness can be any scalar including non-differentiable ones; (b) federated zeroth-order SGD (FedKSeed, DeComFL, SeedFlood) - each client takes many local two-point steps on its own data and ships the (seed, scalar) history. Family (b) tolerates heterogeneous private data and loose synchrony better; family (a) gets a cleaner gradient estimate per step.
- Every billion-parameter result in family (b) is fine-tuning of a pretrained model, where MeZO's theory says pre-training and prompting make the effective dimension small. EGGROLL's finding that population 2 fails for pretraining suggests MeZO-style two-point steps are a poor fit for training a small model from scratch; a real population per step is needed.
- Forward-gradient methods (Baydin et al. 2022 and successors) - background knowledge, not fetched this session - send the same seed plus one scalar (a directional derivative), but computing that scalar needs a forward-mode tangent pass, i.e. a hand-written JVP alongside the forward pass. That is still autograd-free in the "no framework" sense but is extra code per layer, and it does not apply to integer or non-differentiable models, whereas ES does.
- Such et al.'s compact encoding is, from my background knowledge (the abstract does not spell it out), a list of mutation seeds per individual - the earliest "the model is a replayable seed log" idea.

### Gaps
- Forward-gradient accuracy and communication figures were not fetched or verified this session.
- BAFFLE's numbers (forward passes per step, CIFAR accuracy) were not on the abstract page.
- DeepZero's query counts and its first-order baseline number were not extracted.

## 6. Has anyone applied these methods to open, untrusted, peer-to-peer or volunteer swarms? What breaks when a peer lies about its fitness, and what defenses exist?

### Takeaway
I found no work applying EGGROLL or any seed-and-scalar ES to an open, untrusted, serverless browser swarm. The nearest pieces are separate: SeedFlood (serverless but trusting), CYBER-0 / CyBeR-0 (Byzantine-robust zeroth-order, but server-coordinated), and BOINC-era volunteer evolutionary computing (untrusted volunteers, defended by replicated evaluation). EGGROLL itself assumes trusted workers under a coordinator.

### Cited Findings
- EGGROLL's distributed framework is coordinator-worker, with the coordinator maintaining global state; no threat model, authentication or fault handling is discussed. - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix J.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- SeedFlood assumes a static, connected graph with reliable communication and does not discuss Byzantine or malicious peers or any trust model. - [SeedFlood](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.18181v1)
- CYBER-0 (Delgado Neto, Egger, Bakshi, Bitar; June 2024) describes itself as "the first zero-order optimization algorithm for memory-and-communication efficient Federated Learning, resilient to Byzantine faults"; evaluated on MNIST and RoBERTa-Large fine-tuning; convergence guarantees for convex losses. - [arXiv 2406.14362](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14362)
- CyBeR-0 (Egger, Bakshi, Bitar; Jan 2025) uses "transformed robust aggregation" with convergence guarantees for non-convex objectives under heterogeneous client data, and "exhibits stable performance with only a few scalars per-round communication cost". A search snippet notes the perturbation directions must be synchronised across clients. - [arXiv 2502.00193](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00193)
- FedKSeed's paper does not mention Byzantine or malicious clients. - [FedKSeed v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.06353v5)
- Rank-based fitness shaping bounds outliers: in Salimans et al. the rank transformation "removes the influence of outlier individuals in each population". - [Salimans et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.03864)
- EGG's shaping is sign(s_plus - s_minus), so each antithetic pair contributes only a value in {-1, 0, 1}, and a weight moves only when the summed vote passes a z-test threshold that scales with sqrt(N). - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix H.2 and H.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Volunteer computing defences: BOINC uses replication (run a job on two hosts, a third if they disagree) and adaptive replication, which replicates only occasionally for hosts with a good record and "can achieve a low bound on the error rate ... even in the presence of malicious volunteers, while imposing only a small throughput overhead". (From a search summary of the paper; March 2019.) - [BOINC: A Platform for Volunteer Computing](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.01699)
- MilkyWay@Home ran asynchronous evolutionary search on BOINC with unreliable volunteers. A 2009 e-Science paper reports a verification scheme that cuts verification "to less than 30% of the reported results, without degrading the performance of the asynchronous search methods"; a 2010 paper, "Validating Evolutionary Algorithms on Volunteer Computing Grids", presents validation strategies for "unreliable or even partially malicious computing systems". (Search-result excerpts; full texts not retrieved.) - [e-Science 2009](https://computer.org/csdl/proceedings-article/e-science/2009/3877a263/12OmNwnH4SW); [Desell et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-3-642-13645-0_3)
- Removing a participant after the fact: "Efficient Exact Unlearning for Federated Zeroth-Order Optimization" (Yin et al., Oct 2026) exploits the seed-and-scalar log to unlearn a client by keeping earlier updates and restarting from the first affected step; up to 56.1 percent less unlearning time than re-optimisation. (Abstract only.) - [arXiv 2610.35211](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35211)
- General decentralised learning without zeroth-order: BALANCE (Fang et al., 2024) notes "due to its fully decentralized nature, DFL is highly vulnerable to poisoning attacks". (Search snippet only.) - [arXiv 2406.10416](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.10416)

### Inferences
- What a lying peer can do under the plain update theta += alpha/(N sigma) sum F_j eps_j: (1) report an enormous F - the model jumps by an unbounded amount along that peer's random direction, destroying it in one step; (2) negate its true fitness - a cheap, plausible-looking gradient-ascent contribution; (3) report random values without computing anything (free-riding), which adds variance; (4) collude across many identities (Sybil) to dominate the population. Because all seeds are public, an attacker knows every direction, but with k controlled members it can only steer within the span of its own k random directions per step, so precise targeted poisoning is hard while degradation is easy. This analysis is mine; no source tested it for ES.
- Bounded-influence shaping is the first defence and comes free: centred ranks (Salimans) or the sign of an antithetic pair (EGG) cap any one member's influence at that of one honest member. With sign shaping plus EGG's sqrt(N)-scaled vote threshold, flipping a weight requires a coordinated bloc, similar in spirit to majority-vote sign aggregation (the signSGD-with-majority-vote line of work claims Byzantine tolerance below 50 percent adversaries - background knowledge, not fetched).
- Fitness is uniquely cheap to audit in this family, which gradient methods cannot match: the perturbation for (step, member index) is public and deterministic, so if the data batch is also public and the arithmetic is integer (bit-exact across devices), any other peer can recompute a claimed fitness and compare for exact equality. That enables BOINC-style quorum or spot-check replication at a chosen overhead, and with a replayable log (see section 8) a proven liar's contributions can be dropped and the tail of the log re-applied, as in the unlearning paper.
- Exact recomputation fails if fitness depends on a peer's private data or on floating-point paths that differ between browsers and GPUs. With private data, only statistical defences remain: trimmed mean or median over redundant evaluations of the SAME member index by several peers, or CyBeR-0-style robust aggregation over scalars.
- Trimmed means and medians apply naturally when each perturbation is evaluated by several peers on different data (aggregate the scalars for that one direction). They do not help when each direction has a single evaluator; then rank or sign shaping plus auditing is the available tool.
- None of this addresses Sybil resistance or admission control, which are orthogonal to the optimiser.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the full CYBER-0 / CyBeR-0 papers (HTML 404, abstract only), so the specific robust aggregator, the tolerated Byzantine fraction and accuracy under attack are unknown to these notes.
- The Desell et al. validation paper and the 2009 e-Science paper were blocked or not opened; details of their strategies come only from search excerpts.
- I found no empirical study of fitness-lying attacks on OpenAI-style ES or EGGROLL, and no deployment on WebRTC or browser volunteers.

## 7. Does this family work for contrastive or Siamese objectives, and for very small models (under about 1M parameters)?

### Takeaway
For contrastive or Siamese objectives I found no published evidence either way - two targeted searches returned nothing on ES or zeroth-order training of embedding models with contrastive, triplet or InfoNCE losses. For small models there is solid evidence that the family works: EGGROLL's own RL and multi-agent experiments use networks of roughly 10^4 to 10^5 parameters, Salimans used two 64-unit layers, a browser demo trains about 130K parameters, and DeepZero trained ResNet-20 from scratch to 86.94 percent.

### Cited Findings
- EGGROLL was run on small networks: 3-layer MLP policies with 256 hidden units across 16 RL environments, with populations 512 to 4096 and ranks 1, 2 or 4, matching or beating OpenES on 14/16. - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 6.2 and Appendix N.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- EGGROLL multi-agent experiments used feed-forward networks of width 64 and depth 3 with rank 1 and populations of 128, 512 or 4096. - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix N.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- The authors' explanation for where low rank helps: "We postulate that the reason for this performance increase is that the large networks are difficult to optimise for OpenES and lend themselves well to low-rank updates." They also note OpenES is still usable at small network sizes. - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix N.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Salimans et al. trained MuJoCo policies with two 64-unit hidden layers, and observed "slightly better results when using larger networks with ES", hypothesising that large networks have fewer local minima. - [Salimans et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.03864)
- Tapasya trains a from-scratch 4-layer transformer of about 130,000 parameters with EGGROLL in a browser; no quality numbers published. - [Tapasya](https://naklitechie.github.io/Tapasya/)
- FedDisco/DeComFL trained an MNIST CNN with 28,938 parameters from scratch using seed-and-scalar zeroth-order updates, converging more slowly than first-order methods. - [FedDisco v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15861v1)
- DeepZero: ResNet-20 on CIFAR-10 from scratch at 86.94 percent, described as "approaching FO training performance for the first time". - [arXiv 2310.02025](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.02025v4)
- EGGROLL's theory is asymptotic in dimension: rank-1 updates converge to the true ES gradient "as d -> infinity regardless of r", and the linearisation result leans on NTK-style scaling of wide networks. - [arXiv PDF v2, Section 5.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- Closest thing to a contrastive setting found: SharpZO, forward-only prompt tuning of vision-language models using a CMA-ES warm-up followed by sparse zeroth-order updates (June 2025). It tunes prompts for classification on a contrastively pre-trained model; it does not train an embedding model with a contrastive loss. (Search summary only.) - [SharpZO](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2506.20990)
- Two searches for ES / CMA-ES / zeroth-order combined with contrastive loss, triplet loss, InfoNCE, Siamese networks, metric learning or sentence embeddings returned no matching paper. - [Awesome-Gradient-Free-LLMOpt](https://github.com/zz1358m/Awesome-Gradient-Free-LLMOpt) (curated list also contains no such entry in the portion read)

### Inferences
- Nothing in the method excludes contrastive objectives: ES needs only one scalar per perturbed model, and a pairwise or InfoNCE loss over a batch is such a scalar. The Siamese weight tying is automatic because both towers use the same perturbed weights. The open question is purely efficiency, and it is untested in the literature I could find.
- Expected difficulties, by analogy with the EGG ablation: a contrastive loss is noisy in the batch composition (which negatives happen to be present), so the two halves of an antithetic pair must see the identical batch, and the sign-of-difference shaping then cancels most batch noise. With sign shaping each peer can use its own local pairs, because fitness never has to be comparable across peers. With rank shaping across the whole population, all peers would need the same batch.
- Contrastive objectives usually want many negatives per evaluation; in ES every extra example multiplies forward-pass cost by the population size. EGG's finding that near-optimal batch is about N/4096 suggests small batches suffice at moderate populations for next-token loss, but whether that holds for in-batch-negative losses is unknown.
- For models under 1M parameters the low-rank trick is optional: dense perturbations of 10^5 to 10^6 weights are affordable, and the paper itself says OpenES is usable at that scale. Low rank still cuts random-number generation from mn to m+n draws per matrix, which matters in JavaScript or WASM where RNG is likely the dominant cost of reconstructing N perturbations per step.
- Small width weakens the theory's guarantees (they are asymptotic in d), but the empirical record at width 64 and 256 is fine, and the Kaya-Hashemi variance law suggests only a few percent extra variance at those widths. Using rank 2 or 4 for narrow layers is a cheap hedge; the paper's RL search selected ranks 2 and 4 for several environments.
- For tiny models a full-rank requirement is easy to satisfy: N r at least equal to layer width means 64 to 256 members per step.

### Gaps
- No source reports ES, EGGROLL, MeZO or any zeroth-order method training a Siamese, contrastive or metric-learning model, from scratch or fine-tuned. This is an open experimental question, not a known negative.
- No quality numbers exist for the one browser-scale EGGROLL implementation.
- Parameter counts for the RL MLPs are not stated in the paper (they depend on each environment's observation and action sizes).

## 8. Asynchrony: can peers that join late or miss steps catch up by replaying a log of (seed, fitness) pairs? Has anyone described that explicitly?

### Takeaway
Yes, this has been described explicitly in the federated zeroth-order literature - DeComFL/FedDisco has a "ClientRebuildModel" step that replays the seeds and scalars from the rounds a client missed, and FedKSeed compresses the whole history into K accumulators - but not in the EGGROLL paper, which is synchronous. A 2026 study reports ES tolerates stale fitness values well. Nobody has described replay for the integer, thresholded EGGROLL update, where order and exactness matter.

### Cited Findings
- DeComFL/FedDisco: clients that were not sampled rebuild their model by replaying the seeds and gradient scalars from the rounds since they last participated ("ClientRebuildModel"). The authors note this can be computationally demanding for clients unsampled for a long time, and that pulling the full model from the server may be preferable when the client can afford the communication. - [FedDisco v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15861v1)
- FedKSeed: because seeds are restricted to K candidates, the entire history collapses into K scalar accumulators; a client rebuilds the current global model from the initial weights as w = w0 - eta sum_j a_j z_j in at most K operations instead of replaying every step. The paper does not explicitly discuss late-joining clients. - [FedKSeed v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.06353v5)
- SeedFlood: clients "log" new seed-scalar messages and apply them, but the paper assumes a static connected graph and describes no catch-up or replay protocol for late or offline nodes. With delayed flooding, updates arrive with bounded delay of at most ceil(D/k) iterations; performance holds for k of 4 or more flooding steps and drops at k = 1 or 2 (OPT-125M, 32 clients, ring). - [SeedFlood](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.18181v1)
- Staleness tolerance: Hoy et al. (Oct 2026, abstract only) introduce bounded-staleness asynchronous ES for LLM agents on WebShop; "natural asynchrony closely tracks synchronous ES" and "degradation in evaluation accuracy remains small even under extreme staleness", including when all fitness measurements are stale, with no off-policy correction. - [arXiv 2610.04196](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04196)
- Log as a first-class object: Yin et al. (Oct 2026, abstract only) use the seed-and-scalar training record of federated zeroth-order optimisation to perform exact unlearning by keeping earlier updates and restarting from the first affected step. - [arXiv 2610.35211](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35211)
- Salimans et al. 2017 is synchronous per iteration (all returns are exchanged before the update); its only asynchrony-related device is capping episode length to keep CPU utilisation up. - [Salimans et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.03864)
- EGGROLL: the algorithm and the coordinator-worker framework are synchronous; the paper contains no discussion of asynchronous, late-joining or failed workers. Noise is reproducible on demand from a counter-based RNG, and in the integer variant from a pre-generated table of random values. - [arXiv PDF v2, Sections 1, 4.1, Appendices H.1 and J](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)
- EGG integer state is fully integer: int8 weights, int32 accumulations, bit shifts, lookup tables (a division table of shape 2^16 x 2^8; EXP2 and LOG2 tables, with LOG2 precomputed for 2^28 values). - [arXiv PDF v2, Appendix G](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16652v2)

### Inferences
- Replay is mechanically sound for this family: the model at step t is a deterministic function of (initial weights, RNG key, the per-step fitness vectors). A log entry per step is just the packed fitness vector - about N/10 bytes with EGGROLL's ternary antithetic encoding - so a 10,000-step log at population 1,024 is on the order of 1 MB.
- The catch is compute, not bytes, exactly as the DeComFL authors warn: replaying T steps costs T times the per-step update reconstruction, O(N x d) multiply-adds each. For a sub-1M-parameter model a full snapshot is under 1 MB in int8 (under 4 MB in float32) - comparable to or smaller than a long log and far cheaper to apply. The practical design is snapshot plus short log tail: replay only for brief gaps, fetch a peer's snapshot for long absences.
- Replay only converges to the same model if every peer computes bit-identical updates. Floating-point sums over N members can differ across browsers, CPUs and GPUs (ordering, fused multiply-add, WebGPU precision), so float replicas will drift apart. EGG's pure-integer update with a counter-based RNG is exactly reproducible on any platform, which makes it the natural basis for log replay and for cheap state verification by hashing weights. This is my inference; the paper motivates integers by hardware speed and energy, not determinism.
- FedKSeed's accumulator shortcut needs updates that are linear and commutative (plain SGD-style additions). EGG's update is neither - it thresholds a vote and moves weights one bin, with a decaying alpha - so its log must be replayed strictly in order with the exact population membership of each step. Adam-based variants (Appendix K) are also order-dependent and carry extra state.
- "Missed steps" has two meanings. A peer that merely fails to report a fitness is harmless if the step rule allows closing a step with whatever subset arrived (the update just averages fewer members), provided all peers agree on WHICH subset - a consensus problem the literature here does not solve. A peer whose model copy is stale and that evaluates against old weights contributes a stale fitness; Hoy et al. suggest ES tolerates that, but their evidence is fine-tuning large LLMs, not from-scratch small models.
- The EGG lookup tables are a browser concern: a 2^28-entry LOG2 table is hundreds of megabytes at any reasonable entry width. In WASM it would be simpler to compute the fitness logarithm directly; only the weight update and forward pass need to be bit-exact across peers if fitness is audited with a tolerance, or everything if audited by exact equality.

### Gaps
- No paper describes log replay, snapshots or membership agreement for population-based ES in a serverless setting; SeedFlood is the closest and explicitly assumes a static reliable graph.
- The Hoy et al. and Yin et al. findings are abstract-level; staleness bounds, lag values and bookkeeping details were not read.
- No source quantifies cross-device floating-point divergence for ES updates in browsers; the determinism argument above is reasoning, not measurement.
